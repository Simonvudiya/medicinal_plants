Defining Traditional Medicine

Traditional medicine is the sum total of all the knowledge and practices, whether explicable or not, used in the diagnosis, prevention and elimination of physical, mental or social imbalance and relying exclusively on practical experience and observation handed down verbally or in writing from generation to generation.

Traditional medicine might also be considered as a solid amalgamation of dynamic medical know-how and ancestral experience.

Traditional African medicine might also be considered to be the sum total of practices, measures, ingredients and procedures of all kinds, which from time immemorial have enabled the African to guard against disease, to alleviate sufferings, and to cure illness.

The Traditional Healer

A traditional healer is a person who is recognized by the community in which he lives as competent to provide health care by using plant, animal and mineral substances and certain other methods based on the social, cultural and religious background as well as on the knowledge, attitudes and beliefs that are prevalent in the community regarding physical, mental and social well-being and the causation of disease and disability.

Positive Aspects of Traditional Medicine

  1. Traditional medicine is currently being applied to a large proportion of the population in many countries and the very fact that it exists is one of its advantages. Thus, in many localities that are still far from modern health care centres, traditional healers are the only resource when health problems arise. A large number of diseases in the rural areas could be avoided or treated by simple means by people who while not scientifically qualified are within easy reach, like the traditional healers. Furthermore, because of their knowledge and social usefulness, their special relationship with those around them and their understanding of the social and cultural environment, including the patient's family milieu, traditional healers can provide holistic care.
  2. The technical inadequacies of modern medicine and the ever rising cost of modern drugs means that with the possible discovery of new medicaments and broader utilization of the resources of the African pharmacopoeia, the treatment of certain diseases might become more effective and less costly.
  3. Traditional medicine might also be used in the prevention of certain diseases such as schistosomiasis, dracunculiasis, etc. through measures against vectors and parasites.

Negative Aspects of Traditional Medicine

Although the negative features of traditional medicine have been exaggerated by its adversaries in arguments for denying it credibility, it has to be admitted that some aspect of traditional medicine call for corrective measures if it is to be incorporated in the health services. They include:

  1. The imprecise nature of diagnosis.
  2. The lack of precision in dosage.
  3. The possible misuse of its non-material aspects.
  4. The practice of sorcery and quackery.

These negative aspects, however are not peculiar to traditional medicine because even modern medicine has its own disadvantages. It is to make the traditional healers aware of the limits of their skills and to encourage them to cooperate more effectively and dynamically with practitioners of modern medicine.

Major Traditional Medical Practices (or Techniques)

1. Acupuncture

Acupuncture and moxibustion are two methods which have originated in China, for the management of both physical and mental illness. The common factor between these two techniques is that a cure is claimed to be accomplished in either case by stimulating, either separately or simultaneously, one or more of a large number of specific points on the human body.

Acupuncture is the process of piercing the skin at specific points with a sharp needle. The needle is inserted to a predetermined and controlled depth, with specific manipulations, for a definite length of time. The first written reference to acupuncture is said to date back to nearly 2000 years ago. Acupuncture is undoubtedly a unique contribution of Chinese Traditional Medicine to global medicine. The term itself is derived from the Latin words acus (a needle or pin) and puncture (a pricking).

Moxibustion represents a special form of point stimulation. The procedure involves burning a piece of the Chinese drug plant called Artemisia vulgaris (a member of the sunflower group of plants), either on the head of the acupuncture needle so as to conduct heat into the body, or in some cases actually on the surface of the skin. The main material currently in use is moxa wool or moxa wool mixed with Chinese herbs which can stimulate the energy flow in channels and collaterals, ameliorate syndromes arising from cold or wind, and promote the circulation of blood and vital energy.

Ailments that respond to acupuncture:

Although acupuncture and moxibustion are very ancient arts of healing, new experiences are being added continuously and as a consequence, the list of ailments claimed to be curable by these techniques is constantly being updated. The following are some claimed to be conditions that are successfully treated by acupuncture:

  • Neurological disorders: including various types of neuralgia; sensory or motor dysfunction due to organic conditions such as neural paralysis, rheumatism and radicular neuritis with pain, numbness, hyperaesthesia and other features; functional nervous disorders, hysteria, diaphragmatic spasms leading to hiccups; neurasthenia leading to headaches, dizziness, insomnia, etc.
  • Gastrointestinal: Acute gastroenteritis.
  • Menstrual: Irregular and painful menstruation.
  • Respiratory: Bronchial asthma.

The other major application of acupuncture is in relieving surgical pain. It is observed that even the Chinese started using acupuncture to produce analgesia in surgery in 1959 and its use picked up faster since 1968. In certain cases, acupuncture analgesia is used for major surgical operations on the abdomen and thorax. It is also used for such surgical operations as tonsillectomy and tooth extraction among others.

It is reported by some authorities, however, that the widespread assumption that acupuncture is routinely employed for surgery in China is false. Several psychological and environmental factors are said to play a role in the success of acupuncture analgesia in surgery. The Chinese learnt early in life to expect little or no discomfort during surgery, even when surgery is performed without drugs and without acupuncture.

2. Naturopathy or Nature Cure

Naturopathy or Nature Cure which also incorporates Natural Hygiene, may be defined as the science and art of preserving and restoring health by means of those substances, agencies and influences that have a normal relation to life viz. food, air, water, sunlight, exercise, sleep, rest, warmth, cleanliness and such healthful mental influences as hope, faith and peace.

Natural hygienists believe that the primary and universal cause of disease is the presence of toxins in the body tissues (blood, lymph, body fluids and various other components of the body), brought about by ‘enervation’. Enervation is defined as a state of lowered vitality (or nerve energy) which is produced by a mode of living that saps the vitality. Enervation is said to lower the functional power of the body so that secretions and excretions are obstructed, consequently leading to the accumulation of toxins.

Naturopathy believes that one of the most effective methods of accelerating the elimination of toxins and restoring vitality is physiological rest or fasting. This traditional system in its orthodox form is against the use of all drugs, which it considers as ‘poisons’. It also forbids meat-eating and finally believes in a vegetarian diet. Natural Hygiene believes that all diseases (irrespective of their origin) are capable of producing diseases and that therefore ‘poisons’ in the human system. In fact, some naturopaths believe that there are two global medical systems in existence; the Drug Medical System (i.e. all systems which employ drugs to combat diseases) and the Hygienic Medical System which employs normal or hygienic materials and agents. According to Natural Hygiene, the disease process itself is part of nature’s struggle in self-defence and disease is a process of purification. This process should therefore be ‘assisted’ to run its course and not ‘curbed’ or ‘killed’ by drugs. In other words, the naturopath believes in ‘regulating’ the disease and not in subduing it.

Of late, however, some naturopaths have conceded that scientific food selection and combination with simple herb extracts could be used as part of Nature Cure. Some of the modern naturopaths also use chemical and mineral remedies.

Method of Nature Cure:

Nature Cure involves restoration of natural processes by regulated eating, drinking, brushing, bathing, working, resting, thinking and by establishing mental, moral, sexual and social activities on a natural basis. Fasting at regular intervals and regulated diets or monodiets for different diseases form an important part of natural therapy. In monodiets the patient is subjected to period on an exclusive diet of a particular fruit or vegetable or even milk and its products. Monodiet is claimed to be highly beneficial for chronic ailments like rheumatoid arthritis, liver disorders, kidney malfunction and others. Popular monodiets are fruits like grapes, orange, apple, pear, strawberry, pineapple and tomatoes; vegetables like carrots, lettuce, spinach, radish, onions and garlic, potatoes; milk and its natural products like sour milk, yoghurt and butter milk.

Specialized forms of natural therapy:

(a) Osteopathy and Chiropractic: Osteopathy and Chiropractic are special techniques in which manipulation are used in conjunction with certain methods of natural therapy. While the osteopath pays special (although not exclusive) attention to the spinal column, the chiropractor concentrates his treatment entirely on the vertebrae. Alignments of the vertebrae are adjusted by hand, correcting faulty positions and restoring normal mobility. Correction is done by means of thrusts while the patient lie on a table.

(b) Hydrotherapy: Hydrotherapy is a branch of Nature Cure which treats by application of water in various forms i.e. solid, fluid and vapour, both internally and externally. Water may be applied as cold water, hot water, or as alternate hot and cold. Hot baths are claimed to be invariably effective in relieving pain. Cold water is used for improving the tone for the muscles and blood vessels. Alternative hot and cold water application is used for stimulating nutrition and for overcoming local congestion and anaemia without causing any disturbance in the heat balance of the body. In steam and vapour baths, a temperature of 120°F (49°C) is generally employed. In case of Turkish bath or hot air bath, the usual temperature is about 140°-180°F (60°-82°C). Elimination of toxins, through perspiration is said to be effected through these processes and this form of therapy is said to be useful in treating rheumatism, gout, urticaria, diabetes, obesity, etc.

(c) Electrotherapy: Electrotherapy or use of electric current in various forms has been in vogue for some time. The effects produced by electricity may be classified as mechanical, chemical, thermic, actinic and psychic. Heating can also be accomplished by electricity. Diathermy ranks high today among the electro-physical healing processes. The more recent innovation in heat application is ultrasonic or ultrasound therapy. It is claimed that certain conditions like neuralgia which may not respond to prolonged diathermy may derive benefit from ultrasound therapy.

(d) Chromotherapy: Chromotherapy consists mainly in confining the patient to a room in which only one colour or a special blend of primary colours is present. Or in treating parts of the body directly by concentration beams of specific colour(s) on them. Of the various colours, green is said to soothe the nerves, red is used for anaemia, paralysis and other debilitating diseases; reddish orange for cancerous growths, eruptions and other skin diseases; yellowish orange for digestive disorders, pillar box red for sexual disorders in the male and pastel blue for promoting concentration and other mental faculties.

(e) Magnetotherapy: This is a comparatively new technique in naturopathy in which magnets of varying sizes, shape and strength are made to come in contact with selected points in the body (generally the acupuncture points), in order to bring relief from many diseases. Magnetotherapy is being increasingly used by a number of traditional therapists in countries like Egypt, Japan, Russia and U.S.A.

(f) Psychotherapy: Psychotherapy is a part of all therapeutic practices well recognized by modern medicine. Apart from organized psychiatry, all branches of medicine and almost all systems of medicine (traditional or otherwise) make use of psychotherapy in one form or the other. Practices including hypnosis, faith healing, spiritual healing and others also employ psychotherapy.

3. Folk Medicine or Native Medicine including Herbal Remedies

Folk medicine or native medicine in different parts of the world is now being recognized as not only an integral part of the socio-cultural set up of individual population groups and communities but also a source of new knowledge on herbal and other remedies found in nature which are being used by the traditional practitioners on the basis of long standing experience and years of practical observation. The traditional herbalists are part and parcel of the community and are often familiar with the details of each family and its environs so that they are in a better position to deal with their day-to-day problems. These herbalists are highly respected for their superior botanical knowledge and therapeutic skill. In fact, they take care of most of the common ailments of the native folk in their home setting.

Folk medicine in some countries like Africa, Russia, and few European countries is being studied by anthropologists and medical scientist alike. In Russia, in particular, serious attempts have been made in the post-revolution period to scientifically investigate the natural and herbal remedies of folk medicine. It is understood that, in Russia, if a good home remedy is found for any serious ailment, it is usually publicized and praised at the highest medical level. This is indeed a highly commendable trend, but it is not so evident in other parts of the globe. List of popular herbal remedies are available from Africa, Russia and China.

The claim of African traditional medicine to offer herbal drugs for such conditions as herpes zoster (for which modern medicine has no cure), psoriasis, hypertension, bronchial asthma and guinea worm infestation has been clinically proven in a pilot trial. The efforts of WHO in compiling a global inventory of medicinal plants are noteworthy in this context. Among traditional practitioners who seem to offer ‘cures’ from common cold to serious ailments like cancer and several metabolic diseases, the class of healers who claim to treat poisonous snake bites and other similar bites of poisonous insects and animals demand attention. Most folk medicine or native medicine seem to have some remedy or the other for snake bites. Indeed the list of snake bite antidotes in East Africa has increased in this edition.

4. Marginal or Fringe Medicine

It is at present, well-recognized that treatment of illness is not confined to the organized (institutionalized) medical profession alone. Outside the latter, there exist a number of groups of occupational specialists from whom the human race seeks relief for pain and disease. The term ‘Marginal Medicine’ or ‘Fringe Medicine’ has been applied to these traditional practices.

While practices like dianetics and scientology came into prominence only for brief periods in some parts of the globe, certain fringe medicine cults like Christian Science, faith healing and mind cure exist sporadically in various countries. Spiritualistic healing surfaces now and then in certain corners of the world. This type of healing is based on certain beliefs involving extrasensory perception and para-psychology such as the following:

  • Individuals continue after death in disembodied form as spirits;
  • Some human beings, called “mediums” are able to communicate with these spirits and to apply their powers in the living world; and
  • At least some spirits, called “guides” are concerned about the health of living persons and are willing to provide their healing services via a medium.

Spiritualism postulates a co-existent but more fundamental world of the spirit in which things are made up of ‘astral energy’. The task of the healer is said to direct the astral energy under the guidance of the healing spirit. Spiritual healers often believe that they are specially gifted in curing certain diseases, depending upon the expertise and fame of the ‘spirit guide’, when he or she was alive.

Faith healing is an important aspect of all ritualistic practices prevalent in most native populations (including the rural and the tribal). It remains to be decided how much of these practices owe their efficacy (or otherwise) to psycho-analysis and psychotherapy, apart from a pre-conditioning to particular system or practice through generations. It cannot be denied however that most of these so-called ‘Fringe Medicine’ practices have an impact on and a place in the society. When intelligently employed, these may prove to be valuable and powerful tools in both preventive and curative medicine. Further, through proper understanding of their actual impact and mechanisms of efficacy, in specific communities, it might be possible to not only promote such practices as are of beneficial value, but also to avoid those which could be harmful.

Summary of African Traditional Practitioners

  • (a) Herbalists: This category of traditional healers usually use plants for treating their patients. The most widely recognised traditional practitioners.
  • (b) Diviners: Are also herbalists but use divinatory procedures for treatment.
  • (c) Spiritualists: Hardly use plants at all for treatment.
  • (d) Great Therapists: Utter prayers, incantations and invocations and use these as weapons against powerful, supernatural or invisible forces. They give people “vitalized” charms, amulets, bracelets, belts and pendants of all kinds to protect them against diseases, accidents and bewitchment.
  • (e) Traditional Midwives (Birth Attendants): May also be obstetricians, herbalists, gynaecologists or paediatricians. They provide healthcare before, during and after birth, and also cares for new born infants and young children.
  • (f) Traditional Surgeons: Use special knives, sharpened and tempered according to esoteric procedures for circumcision and excisions. Cassava leaves, liquid from herbs and various other ingredients are used as agents to check haemorrhage.
  • (g) Traditional Psychiatrists: Deal with the mental patient’s socio-religious antecedents. They have a series of rites which include charms, incantations and ritual dances whereby music is played using certain specific musical instruments.
