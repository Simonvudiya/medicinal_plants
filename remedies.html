Part of the Plant Used

This varies from one species to another and also depends upon the structure of the plant. For trees and shrubs, the common practice is to use the roots or sometimes both. Less commonly used in this group are the leaves, while flowers or fruits (including seeds) are rarely used. It is known in East Africa that the drug plants are generally referred to as “dawa ya miti” (Swahili) meaning “medicine of trees”, or colloquially as “miti shamba”. This indicates how widely trees and shrubs are generally used as sources of local drugs. The Swahili word “miti” is however, generally used to refer to any stem of the plant.

Among the herbaceous plants, the use of grasses for medicinal purposes is very rare. With herbaceous plants generally, there is a tendency to uproot and use the whole plant. In contrast with trees, the leaves of herbaceous plants are most widely used, followed by their roots. With the succulents, the leaves and whole stems are commonly used, especially in cases where the treatment requires a poultice.

Preparation of Drugs

There are several methods of preparing drug plants or their parts before they are given to a patient. One of the common methods is by boiling the plant parts. Roots and bark of trees are commonly boiled and the resulting decoction used either internally (orally) or externally. Leaves are generally pounded and then soaked in cold or warm water and the infusion used either internally or externally. This second method of soaking in cold water may occasionally be used in preparing root or stem drugs.

The third method is by burning the whole plant or just its useful parts. This method is common with leaves and small herbs. The common practice is to dry the plant or its part before burning it. The resultant ash may be applied to the affected body in several ways as will be described under methods of application.

Pounding is a procedure which normally precedes other methods like boiling, soaking or burning, depending on the structure of the plant part being dealt with. In some cases however, the plant or its parts may be pounded and applied directly to the infection, thus it becomes the fourth method of preparation. Such preparations are widely used externally for complaints such as boils, wounds and other skin diseases. Pounded stuff is frequently mixed with some kind of cream, particularly ghee or covered with some sort of bandage.

The fifth method of preparation is more of a first-aid technique than just a method for preparing a drug. Chewing is a quick method, and is most often used to extract juice for immediate application.

Application of Drugs

There are many methods of applying or administering the prepared drugs to the patient, and these depend largely on the particular disease to be treated. The application also follows closely the method by which the drug has been prepared. Juice from boiled stuff is frequently drunk for internal treatment, used as bathing water for external diseases, or drunk and used at the same time for bathing. These same methods are used in applying soaked drugs.

Pounded stuff, unless further boiled or soaked, is commonly mixed with ghee and simply rubbed on the affected part. Alternatively it may not be mixed with ghee but is simply rubbed on the affected part or tied with a bandage to support it. Sometimes the affected part of the body is cut open with a razor blade and then the medicine is rubbed in the small opening.

The purpose of chewing a drug plant is to extract its juice. The extracted juice can be swallowed if it is for internal treatment or simply retained in the stomach, spat on the wound if it is for external application, or such as stomachache, spot on the long period if it is for some kind of mouth complaint such as toothache.

A number of aromatic drugs are used in steam form, by first boiling them, and then letting the patient inhale the vapour. Ash from burned drugs is commonly licked for internal treatment, applied directly on the wound if open or on small cuts (usually wherever the pain is located) by the razor blade, or rarely, soaked in water and drunk. In most cases the ash is mixed with salt. Roasted succulent leaves are mainly applied externally by pressing them on the affected part of the body and constantly reheating them to keep them hot. This process is known as poulticing.

Quite often the drugs are mixed with food and eaten. The Maasai for example are fond of taking their drugs in the milk, while the Kamba frequently take theirs in the soup. Another base food frequently used by East Africans for taking various drugs is gruel, preferably that made from "wimbi" (African millet) or maize flour.

Negative Aspects of Traditional Medicine

Although the negative features of traditional medicine have been exaggerated by its adversaries in arguments for denying it credibility, it has to be admitted that some aspect of traditional medicine call for corrective measures if it is to be incorporated in the health services. They include:

  • The imprecise nature of diagnosis.
  • The lack of precision in dosage.
  • The possible misuse of its non-material aspects.
  • The practice of sorcery and quackery.

These negative aspects, however are not peculiar to traditional medicine because even modern medicine has its own disadvantages. It is to make the traditional healers aware of the limits of their skills and to encourage them to cooperate more effectively and dynamically with practitioners of modern medicine.

Major Traditional Medical Practices

1. Acupuncture

Acupuncture and moxibustion are two methods which have originated in China, for the management of both physical and mental illness. The common factor between these two techniques is that a cure is claimed. Acupuncture is the process of piercing the skin at specific points with a sharp needle. The needle is inserted to a predetermined and controlled depth, with specific manipulations, for a definite length of time. Moxibustion represents a special form of point stimulation, involving burning a piece of the Chinese drug plant called Artemisia vulgaris.

Chemical Constituents of the Drug Plant

Sulphur Oils

Continuing with the oils, we now come to the group called Sulphur oils, which are usually found in seeds of Boscia, Capparis, Cleome, and also in members of the Cruciferae. These oils differ from the essential oils only in their composition, being sulphur compounds. They are all very irritating and usually cause reddening and even vesiculation of the skin, and also act as carminatives in small doses or as emetics in large doses. Other sulphur oils are found in the species of Capsicum and Salvadora.

Resins

Resins are fats which are solid, the majority of which are extremely irritating. Some resins cause vomiting and purging if taken in large doses. Many gumresins can be collected from the local species of Boswellia and Commiphora. It is also believed that resins may be part of the milky latex of Calotropis species. Many resins resemble the essential oils in their drug activities, for example the local Piper and Zingiber are used as carminatives, while Albizia and Zanthoxylum species are widely used for the urinary tract. The purgative resins obtained from Ipomoea species are considered very effective. The adhesive quality of resins gives them value as wound-dressing material. One should not forget that there are some poisonous resins, a couple of them being used locally as arrow poisons.

Glycosides

GLYCOSIDES are compounds made of hexose sugars (mainly glucose). As drug plant constituents, they will be subdivided further into more convenient groups. Tannins are glycosides of gallic or protocatechuic acids. They have the property of precipitating proteins and mucus and also constricting blood vessels. Such astringent property of the tannins gives them the medicinal value of preventing diarrhoea and controlling haemorrhage. They may also be applied to wounds as a protective coating. Tannins are among the most abundant of the active principles in plants, particularly in trees and shrubs where they are mainly found in the bark. There are many trees in East Africa whose bark contains large quantities of tannins, among them the species of Acacia, Diospyros, Kigelia, Pterocarpus, Rhizophora, Spathodea, and others. Several plants containing tannins are used as vermifuges and for controlling diarrhoea. Since tannins are mainly from the bark of trees, they are usually extracted by boiling the bark in water or soaking it in cold water.

The local “medicine” men have not yet fully recognized the use of the Cardiac Glycosides contained primarily in the Apocynaceae. The poisonous use of the Acokanthera species is, on the other hand, well known. There are other glycosides of lesser importance, most of them poisons.

Alkaloids

ALKALOIDS normally exist in plants as salts of various organic acids and amine bases. Such salts are colourless crystalline compounds. Although the more active alkaloids are among the most potent vegetable poisons, a good number of them can be taken in fairly large doses without danger. Most of our plants with active alkaloids are frequently used as poisons rather than as drugs. Such toxic alkaloids as exemplified by Datura stramonium, normally affect the nervous system. The less toxic alkaloids such as caffeine, and sparteine would normally increase the renal secretion either by increasing the blood flow through the kidneys or by some direct action and are thus used as diuretics and in the treatment of dropsy. Most alkaloids are characterized by a bitter taste, however, and their value as drugs is still little known.

Toxalbumins

TOXALBUMINS are poisonous proteins, usually irritant in nature, and mainly found in the seeds of plants. They can induce inflammation of the mucous membranes such as those of the eye or nose, and can cause violent vomiting and purging when swallowed since they are not digested. Powder or extracts of seeds from Abrus precatorius, Cassia abusu, and Croton spp. have been known to be applied to ulcers or to congested eyes to induce an inflammatory reaction followed by healing. Toxalbumins have also been reported in various species of the Euphorbiaceae, for example it is found in Castor oil and Jatropha oil.

Benzoquinones

BENZOQUINONES. The anthelmintic action of the genera Embelia, Maesa, Myrsine and Rapanea, all members of the Myrsinaceae has now been established to be due to alkylbenzoquinones like maesaquinone and embelin. The berries of Maesa lanceolata contain about 15% benzoquinones and embelin. The berries of Rapanea melanophloeos have about 10% benzoquinones.

Anthraquinone Cathartics

ANTHRAQUINONE CATHARTICS may be found in plants in combined or as glycosides. Cassia (Leguminosae) and Aloe (Aloaceae) are the most important genera containing these type of compounds. Other local genera with these compounds are Rhamnus and Rumex. All these genera have species which are used as cathartics, while others are used as dressings for burns and other skin lesions. Cassia and Senna species have pods which contain emodin, barks which may yield tannin, and most important are their leaves which yield senna.

Summary of East African Drug Plants

A large number of plant species in the savanna and semi-arid areas of East Africa contain alkaloids. The tomato or Sodom apple family, Solanaceae is the richest source of alkaloids. The Datura species of the same family contain the important alkaloid groups called hyoscine and hyoscyamine. Solanum species contain several steroidal alkaloids which are also glycosidal in character.

Ranking next to the alkaloids as an important chemical constituent of East African drug plants are the essential oils. These are mostly found here in the mint family, Labiatae. Essential oils are also found in members of the carrot family, Umbelliferae. Aloe species in the Aloaceae contain an aloin-cathartic principle, while several Acacia species in the Leguminosae are known to yield valuable medicinal gums. An interesting variation in chemical constituents occurs in Euphorbia species, most of which are reported to be toxic, but are also used as herbal drugs by some ethnic groups.
