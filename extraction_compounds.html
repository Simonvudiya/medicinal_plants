Extracting compounds from medicinal plants

A practical guide to the full workflow: from collecting the plant to isolating and identifying a pure compound. It explains what the silica gel, TLC plates and solvents on a typical procurement list are for and how they fit together.

Overview of the workflow

A crude plant extract holds dozens to hundreds of compounds. The goal is to separate them step by step, using differences in polarity, until single compounds can be tested and identified.

  1. Collect and authenticate the plant, record voucher details
  2. Dry and grind stabilise and increase surface area
  3. Extract move compounds into a solvent to give a crude extract
  4. Fractionate split the crude extract by polarity
  5. TLC see how many compounds there are and find a solvent system
  6. Column chromatography separate the fraction on silica
  7. TLC monitoring combine fractions with matching patterns
  8. Final purification Sephadex, preparative TLC, HPLC, recrystallisation
  9. Identify NMR, MS, UV, FTIR
Bioassay-guided isolationFor medicinal work, each extract or fraction is usually tested in a biological assay (antibacterial, antioxidant, cytotoxicity and so on). Only the active fractions are carried forward, so effort is spent on compounds that matter.

1. Plant selection and preparation

Selection and authentication

  • Choose plants from ethnobotanical use, literature or previous screening hits.
  • Have a botanist identify the species. Deposit a voucher specimen in a herbarium and record the voucher number.
  • Record collection date, place (GPS), plant part, season and growth stage. Chemistry varies with all of these.
  • Obtain collection permits and follow access and benefit-sharing rules (for example under the Nagoya Protocol) before collecting.

Drying

  • Dry in shade with good airflow, or in an oven at 30–40 °C. Strong sun and high heat can destroy sensitive compounds.
  • Spread material thinly and turn it. It is dry when it snaps or crumbles.
  • For very heat-labile or volatile compounds, freeze-dry or extract fresh material.

Grinding and storage

  • Grind to a coarse-to-medium powder. Too fine clogs filters and columns; too coarse extracts poorly.
  • Weigh the powder, because yields are reported as % of dry weight.
  • Store in labelled, airtight containers, away from light and moisture.

3. Extraction methods

Extraction moves compounds from the plant matrix into a solvent. Choose a method based on the stability of the compounds, the scale and the equipment available.

Typical maceration procedure

  1. Weigh the dry powder (for example 100 g to 1 kg).
  2. Place in a clean flask or container and cover with solvent, typically 5–10 times the powder weight in volume (for example 1 L per 100–200 g).
  3. Seal and leave 24–72 h at room temperature, shaking occasionally.
  4. Filter through cloth or filter paper. Keep the filtrate.
  5. Re-extract the residue (marc) two or more times with fresh solvent and pool the filtrates.
  6. Concentrate under reduced pressure on a rotary evaporator (water bath around 40 °C) until a syrupy or solid residue remains.
  7. Dry completely (desiccator, vacuum or freeze-drier), weigh, and calculate % yield.
% yield = (mass of dry crude extract ÷ mass of dry plant powder) × 100

Sequential extraction

The same powder is extracted with solvents of increasing polarity, for example hexane, then DCM, then ethyl acetate, then methanol. Each extract holds a chemically different group of compounds, and fractionation is built into the extraction.

Single-solvent extraction

Extract once with methanol (or ethanol) to capture a broad range, then partition the crude extract between solvents. Faster, and common when screening many plants.

4. Fractionation of the crude extract

Liquid–liquid partitioning

  1. Dissolve or suspend the crude methanol extract in water, or in methanol–water (for example 9:1).
  2. Transfer to a separatory funnel and add an immiscible solvent, starting with the least polar (hexane or petroleum ether).
  3. Shake gently with venting, let the layers separate, and collect the organic layer. Repeat 3 times.
  4. Repeat with the next solvent in order of polarity (DCM or chloroform, then ethyl acetate, then butanol if wanted).
  5. Evaporate each solvent layer separately. The leftover aqueous layer is the most polar fraction.

This gives, for example, a hexane fraction (fats, waxes, non-polar terpenes), a DCM or chloroform fraction (moderately non-polar compounds), an ethyl acetate fraction (phenolics and flavonoids) and an aqueous fraction (very polar compounds and sugars).

Defatting

Leaf and seed material often holds large amounts of chlorophyll, fats and waxes that foul columns. A preliminary hexane or petroleum ether wash removes much of this before the main extraction.

5. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)

TLC is the everyday tool for seeing what is in a sample. It uses silica on a flat support, with a solvent that moves up by capillary action.

What TLC is used for

  • Counting roughly how many compounds an extract or fraction holds
  • Comparing fractions to see if they match
  • Choosing the solvent system for a column
  • Monitoring column fractions
  • Checking the purity of an isolated compound

Silica powder vs ready-made plates

  • TLC-grade silica powder is used to coat plates in the lab (and sometimes for preparative plates).
  • Ready-made plates (silica on aluminium, glass or plastic) are convenient and more uniform for routine work.
  • Confirm the exact intended use against the procurement specification.

Running a TLC plate

  1. Draw a faint pencil line about 1 cm from the bottom. Never use pen, as the ink will run.
  2. Dissolve a little sample in a volatile solvent and spot it on the line with a capillary. Keep spots small. Let them dry.
  3. Pour solvent into a chamber to about 0.5 cm depth (below the spot line). Line with filter paper and close the lid so the air saturates.
  4. Place the plate in the chamber and let the solvent rise until about 1 cm from the top. Remove and mark the solvent front immediately.
  5. Dry the plate and visualise (see below).
Rf = distance travelled by the compound ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front

For example, a spot that moves 3.6 cm when the solvent front has moved 7.2 cm has an Rf of 0.50. Strongly retained compounds have a low Rf; weakly retained, mobile ones have a high Rf. Aim for Rf values between about 0.2 and 0.5 for the compounds you want to separate on a column.

6. Column chromatography on silica

The same polarity logic as TLC, scaled up. Silica is the stationary phase. Non-polar compounds move quickly and polar ones are held back.

Procedure

  1. Choose the solvent system. Use TLC to find a mixture where the target compounds have Rf about 0.2–0.4. Start the column with a weaker (less polar) solvent than that.
  2. Select the amount of silica. A common rule is 30 to 100 g silica per 1 g sample (more for difficult separations). Use a column long and narrow enough for the separation.
  3. Pack the column. Wet packing is most reliable: make a slurry of silica in the starting solvent, pour it in, tap to remove air, and let it settle. Never let the silica run dry or crack.
  4. Load the sample. Either dissolve it in a minimum of solvent and apply a narrow band, or dry-load by adsorbing it onto a little silica, evaporating the solvent, and adding the powder to the top. Add a thin layer of sand to protect the surface.
  5. Elute. Run isocratically (one solvent mixture) or by gradient, increasing polarity in steps, for example hexane, then hexane : ethyl acetate (9:1, 8:2, 1:1), then ethyl acetate, then ethyl acetate : methanol.
  6. Collect fractions of consistent volume in numbered tubes or flasks.

Monitoring and combining fractions

Spot every few fractions on one TLC plate alongside the starting material. Fractions with the same spot pattern are pooled and evaporated. Label everything clearly, because mistakes here are hard to undo.

Gravity column

Simple and cheap. Solvent drips through by gravity. Slower, but ideal for large amounts and teaching labs.

Flash column

Gentle air or nitrogen pressure speeds the flow, giving faster and often sharper separations. Pre-packed cartridge systems are also available.

Vacuum liquid chromatography (VLC)

A quick first split of a large crude extract into several polarity fractions under vacuum, before finer columns.

Checking purity: a single spot in several different TLC solvent systems, a sharp melting point (for crystals), a single peak by HPLC, and clean NMR spectra all support purity.

9. Tips by compound class

Alkaloids

Basic nitrogen compounds. A classic acid–base method: extract with dilute acid (they dissolve as salts), wash with a non-polar solvent to remove neutrals, make the aqueous layer basic with ammonia, then extract the free bases into DCM or chloroform.

Flavonoids and phenolics

Aglycones go into ethyl acetate; glycosides stay more polar (methanol, aqueous). Sephadex LH-20 and C18 are useful. They oxidise easily, so avoid prolonged heat and light.

Terpenoids and sterols

Found in hexane, DCM and ethyl acetate fractions. Essential oils come from hydro-distillation. Silica columns with hexane and ethyl acetate gradients work well.

Saponins and glycosides

Polar, often in butanol and aqueous fractions. They foam when shaken. Defat first, then use methanol or ethanol–water, and often reversed-phase or Diaion/HP-20 resin.

Tannins

Large polyphenols soluble in water and aqueous acetone. Sephadex LH-20 is the usual method.

Coumarins and quinones

Medium polarity, usually in DCM and ethyl acetate fractions. Many fluoresce under UV on TLC, which helps tracking.

10. Safety, quality and record keeping

Solvent hazardsHexane, petroleum ether and diethyl ether are highly flammable. Ether can form explosive peroxides. DCM and chloroform are toxic, and chloroform is a suspected carcinogen. Methanol is toxic by ingestion, skin contact and inhalation. Work in a fume hood, away from ignition sources, and never heat solvents with an open flame.

Protection

  • Lab coat, safety glasses and suitable gloves (nitrile is not suitable for all solvents, so check compatibility)
  • Good ventilation or fume hood
  • Silica dust is hazardous when inhaled: avoid creating dust, and wear a mask when handling dry powder

Waste

  • Keep halogenated (DCM, chloroform) and non-halogenated waste separate
  • Label containers and follow institutional disposal rules
  • Recover solvents by rotary evaporation where permitted

Records

  • Keep a lab notebook: plant details, masses, solvent volumes, dates, yields
  • Label every flask, vial and TLC plate (and photograph plates)
  • Store extracts cold, dry and dark, with a clear sample ID
  • Handle unknown plant extracts as potentially toxic
