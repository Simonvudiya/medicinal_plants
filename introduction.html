The Use of Plants in East Africa

The use of plants for the treatment of various diseases, as a specific antidote against magic, and for religious ceremonies is universal and has been practiced for many years. The East African herbalist has often been referred to as “Bwana mganga” (Swahili) or by similar terminology in other languages to mean “medicine man”. We all know that people were living in various parts of Africa long before the arrival of the early European explorers and missionaries. It is also true that these people, together with their domestic animals, from time to time became sick or were injured under their existing environmental conditions. Even now when many people are being treated with modern drugs, East Africans, both literate and illiterate, still use local drug plants in many ways.

For many years, our people have developed a store of empirical information concerning the therapeutic value of local plants. Some of the plants used for direct or specific treatment of particular diseases are worth further investigation in order to determine their chemical composition and their reaction on the diseases in question. We often hear of our leaders disapproving of the use of local plants and advising people to use modern medical facilities. While I agree with this advice, I would appeal to modern scientists not to ignore the whole field of local drug plants. I have noticed that in most cases the bad results obtained through the use of local plant drugs are basically due to over-dosage and also to lack of adequate knowledge of other detrimental by products contained in the same plants. The modern East African scientist knows very well that a locally brewed gin known as “kill me quick” or “chang’aa” is generally poorly distilled, and contains other products such as methanol which are injurious to human body cells and the nervous system. Too much of something is always dangerous, therefore too much of either an imported or local drink or a drug may harm the body. It would be most appropriate and rewarding if modern chemical analysis could be applied in order to detect the medicinal values of those plants reported to be of use.

Traditional Knowledge and Practitioners

The art of native medicine has been practiced in East Africa for many years and is still being widely used even today. The knowledge of medicinal plants is normally passed on orally from one generation to the next. But a lot of valuable information can be lost or distorted whenever a medicine man dies without revealing his knowledge to another. A major problem for our medicine men is to keep the knowledge secret and important only to trustworthy persons. Such knowledge is normally transmitted when the father is getting old or is just about to die. The son or other persons inheriting the knowledge will normally take an oath not to reveal its secrets to anybody else. Instructions are generally given in the field, where the son is shown the plant, told its vernacular name, and told how to prepare the drug from the plant or plant part for specific diseases. The sequence of passing on the knowledge therefore requires the recipient to have a good memory. Occasionally, the number of plants and diseases treated are too many for the inheriting “doctor” to remember them all, thus resulting in one source of errors or loss of knowledge.

Some medicine men unfortunately, keep the secrets to themselves until the last minute before death when they call whoever is to inherit the knowledge to give instructions. This is a dangerous method of passing on knowledge since the recipient may easily forget or misunderstand the information. For many years, a man’s wealth in East Africa has been measured both by the number of his wives and the number of his cattle. I have noted that some medicine men pass on their knowledge to their first wives. Such women usually dominate the field of child diseases and also female medicine, particularly that pertaining to pregnancy and childbirth. It is by this method that mothers frequently pass on plant drugs knowledge to their daughters.

One factor that tends to bring herbal medicine into disrepute is the desire among some practitioners to become wealthy by pretending to know the remedy for every disease their patients may complain of. In many instances such medicine men take money for what they cannot treat and keep on administering one drug after another. Such treatment frequently leads to the death of the patient, thus the public begin to react negatively towards both the “doctor” and the art of local plant medicine. Contrarily, a number of genuine plant doctors will normally specify the type of diseases they can actually treat and will not accept payment for what they do not know. The trend with such genuine herbal doctors is to accept a nominal payment at the time of treatment and the proper payment later when the patient has been cured.

The treatment of bewitched persons and the removal of evils or misfortunes from houses are special ceremonies normally performed during the night. Such ceremonies usually involve more than just the use of plants. In most cases they also involve animal life and equipment such as a pot, spear, hoe, quail’s nest, and so on.

Chemical Constituents of the Drug Plant

Since the publication of the first and second editions of this book in 1976 and 1993 respectively, a substantial amount of research on the chemical constituents of our medicinal plants has been undertaken, especially after 1980. Both local and overseas natural product chemists have explored through research, the active principles contained in these plants and it is hoped they will continue to utilize the material in this third edition to further research activities in this vital area of our drug plants.

I should like to mention here some basic compounds commonly associated with plants and which are reported to have medicinal value. Oils or FATS are common constituents of a number of drug plants. The first group of oils that one finds in drug plants are those which do not distil at the temperature of boiling water we have seen that water is a common medium for preparation of local drugs. Such oils are called fixed oils, and consist of molecules of fatty acid which form some kind of salt or ester with one molecule of glycerine. The fixed oils of Annona, Balanites and Trichilia species are used both as emollients and as ointment bases. The Euphorbiaceae are characterized by strongly unsaturated fatty acids which are not digested or absorbed. Members of this family, such as the Castor oil plant (Ricinus communis), and the Croton species have seeds which are used as mild or strong purgatives. But both types of plant oils contain toxalbumins which should be destroyed by heat before use. Ximenia species also contain cathartic fixed oils.

Another group of oils common in drug plants are the Essential oils, which are volatile and usually odorous liquids due to the scents of flowers or other plant parts from which they derive. These are usually non-nitrogenous compounds, most of them liquid, and have a pleasant smell. One interesting aspect of these oils is that they can regulate the intestinal movements, preventing or controlling violent contractions and aiding the orderly flow of food through the bowel. Plants with these oils are, therefore, widely used as condiments with food and also to relieve colicky pain. It has been noticed that many essential oils have the power to hinder bacterial growth, thus plants with such essential oils are generally used for treating wound infections. Oils which are less well absorbed may be of value as vermifuges, frequently take theirs in tea. Various types of African beer are also used for taking plant drugs.

Therapeutic Properties and Classification

The therapeutic or medicinal properties of plants are normally dependent upon the presence of certain active principles. In studying plants we find that they contain a structural framework based on cellulose and lignin and the important cells contain protoplasm which in turn contains chlorophyll, starches, sugars, and inulins. Plants may also contain other much rarer substances which vary from plant to plant and whose influence or reaction in an animal body is also variable. Depending on the plant one uses, plants can be nutritive, poisonous, or medicinal. Just as research has been done to prove that maize contains starch and beans have protein needed by our bodies, drug plants also need some research done on them to prove or disprove their therapeutic values.

Drug plants may be broadly classified into three major categories as follows: first, botanically according to the species from which drugs are obtained, secondly according to the purpose for which a species is used, and finally according to the active chemicals of the species. Part one of this book is therefore based upon the first method of classification which is botanical, giving to each species only its medical value. The second part is much of a reference to what is in part one, and in this classification each disease has listed under it all plant species reported to cure it. The third category of the above classification, the chemical nature, which is important but requires more time to study and will only be mentioned briefly. I have for example, noted that Embelia, Maesa and Myrsine, all members of the family Myrsinaceae are reported as being used in treating intestinal worms. This indicates the presence of some common active principle in the family which has recently been identified as alkylbenzoquinones like maesaquinone and embelin.
